LEAD CASE 1Helpdesk AILive in UK, Middle East, ANZBerkeley (UAE) · Q3 Services (UK)Owner, end to endVoice · WhatsApp · Email · Web

An AI helpdesk that never closes

In facilities management, every repair starts with someone reporting a problem. Across my discovery calls the same picture kept coming back: after-hours requests go nowhere, and the whole intake desk often depends on one person.

At Berkeley Services in the UAE it runs as Al Najah: email, WhatsApp and calls around the clock, urgent issues escalated straight away. At Q3 Services in the UK it runs as Bernard, taking requests by voice, email and WhatsApp.

I designed Helpdesk AI as a set of agents, each doing one job a helpdesk operator does today: take the request, try a quick fix, route it, pick the technician, confirm the visit, chase the follow-ups, and ask the customer how it went.

What I heard

Out of 80 discovery calls in four months, two choke points showed up everywhere: getting a request into the system, and getting an invoice approved. That set the roadmap. Helpdesk AI first, Payment Validator next.

At Berkeley the case for intake was in their own numbers: 88% of contacts were new jobs, 10% technician follow-ups and 1% status checks. Each new job took an agent about 3 minutes to type. That is roughly 80 staff hours a quarter now spent on site work instead.

What one night looks like
2:17 AM. A tenant calls about a water leak. The agent answers, takes the issue and location, and mid-call sends a WhatsApp asking for a photo. It reads the photo, marks it urgent plumbing, creates the request and sends the reference number back. About 90 seconds. Nobody had to wake up.
What broke after go-live
  • Names. Speech-to-text heard "Al Furjan" as "Alpha John", so requests landed on the wrong site. We gave the speech engine all 144 Berkeley site names as words to listen for, and checked each result against the full site list.
  • Noise. The agent asked "What is your name?", a child in the background said "Hey Mom", and "Mom" became the caller's name. Every detail is now read back to the caller before the request is raised.
  • Voice manners. Cutting people off, not waiting, not following the script. Many nights of testing went into this, and some of it still shows up in production.
What I'd do differently

Start with a voice that sounds local. A Saudi accent is not a UAE accent, and you can't sound American in the UK. We learned it in production; Berkeley moved to a female voice that speaks both Arabic and English.

Calls I had to make
  • Speed vs thinking time, per channel. A tenant on a phone call won't wait. An email agent can take longer and reason more.
  • Where the human stays. Emergencies and categories the customer names go straight to a person. That path was designed in from day one.
  • Prompt vs pipeline. If a rule needs memory the model doesn't have, like "stop replying to auto-replies", it lives in the pipeline, not the prompt.
  • Pricing. We tried per seat and prepaid credits, dropped both, and landed on paying per action. Outcome pricing per dispatched job where the outcome is measurable.
Learning from the market

Before designing setup, I walked through five AI support products end to end and noted how each one handles onboarding, knowledge sources, human handoff, testing and analytics. That shaped a self-serve setup where a facility team answers a few plain questions (what you manage, what to do with requests you don't handle, what counts as an emergency, what every caller must give) and gets a working agent.

What counts as a failure

I keep a fixed list of what goes wrong in AI conversations: no reply, slow replies, made-up facts, repeating itself, missing frustration, a bad greeting or goodbye, a broken handoff, not collecting enough detail, wrong tone, interrupting, and the big one, not actually doing the job.

How I test before customers touch it

Misheard words get their own test: if the agent isn't sure what it heard ("panel" or "fan"), it says back what it heard so the caller can correct it, and never logs a guess. 50+ real scenarios per agent, scored against the actual records in the system, not the reply text. Every prompt change is versioned and re-tested, and old chat sessions are cleared for every contact before anyone tests again.

LEAD CASE 2Field agents on WhatsAppLiveQ3 Services (UK) · CIT Group (Saudi Arabia)Product lead

Putting the person who did the job back on the record

Technicians and site supervisors don't log into a portal. They live on WhatsApp. So the record of a visit, the attendance, the checklist, the photos, the certificate, gets typed in later by someone in the office who wasn't there.

Before building Bruce, the technician agent for Q3 Services in the UK, I read a year of their own records to see how big the gap really was. For CIT Group in Saudi Arabia, I built a supervisor agent so site supervisors raise jobs and add after-photos from WhatsApp.

1.2%→7.9%of reactive work orders raised by CIT site supervisors, once the full loop was in WhatsApp
189work orders raised and closed in WhatsApp by 6 site supervisors in 9 weeks
60%of Q3 jobs still being edited a week after the work ended, before Bruce

1.2% → 7.9%: CIT site, Jan–Jul 2026 baseline vs August 2026. 189: CIT export, 30 Jul – 3 Oct 2026, test records removed; 86% closed, 66% within SLA. 60%: Q3 records before go-live.

The head asked for one thing. The ground said another. A CIT head wanted supervisors to create work orders in WhatsApp, nothing more. I built that, and the needle didn't move. Then I spoke to the supervisors. Jobs were still being closed, by the helpdesk team in the Facilio app, as extra work on top of their own. So I built the whole loop myself in a few days: create, start, complete, all in WhatsApp. Within weeks the supervisors' share went from 1.2% to 7.9%. I showed the head the numbers, and he agreed the full loop was the better answer.
Read the full story
What the records showed
  • The role for site operatives existed in the system, with nobody assigned to it.
  • Only about a quarter of jobs were closed off on the day. A third were still changing a month later.
  • 62% of on-demand jobs had no request behind them, because phone and email requests never became records.

That gave us a baseline to measure against, set before the agent went live. Targets were left for the customer to set.

What the agents do

The technician agent walks the job as the technician lives it: accept or reject, attendance, insurance and water-risk questions, checklist and meter readings, further works, the compliance date, the service certificate filed straight from a WhatsApp photo, then resolve.

The supervisor agent lets supervisors raise a work order from the field and attach after-photos as proof of completion.

A rule I hold to

The state rules live in the platform, not the prompt. Seven functions check the record before every move and refuse illegal ones. A job can't be resolved without its checklist, insurance answers and, on planned work, its compliance date.

How I build an agent

I start from the flow, the people doing it today and the baseline numbers. The steps are fixed; the model handles the language. I choose the model, write the prompt, define the functions it can call and decide when each one is called.

What I'd do differently

Talk to the people on the ground before the first build, not after. Asking only the head of the account cost us a round.

Watch for: the form opens inside WhatsApp; after saving, "Create another work order" or close and you're back in the chat. In Bruce, Next jumps straight to the next reading, nothing is saved without a confirm step, and "Back to chat with Bruce" returns the technician to the conversation.

LEAD CASE 3Payment ValidatorLive in AustraliaOwner, end to endAustralian property group

Every invoice checked before anyone approves it

Invoice approval was the second choke point from discovery. Facility managers were opening every vendor invoice and checking it by hand against the work order and the purchase order: is the business number right, does the invoice number match, does the amount match, is it addressed to us, is it a proper tax invoice.

Payment Validator reads the PDF, compares it with the record and shows the result side by side. Hard failures are rejected automatically and the vendor is emailed. Clean invoices are marked safe to approve. The manager only looks at what needs a human.

Two kinds of checks

Hard checks compare a field on the PDF with the same field in the system: business number, invoice number, amount before tax. A mismatch stops the invoice. Soft checks read the document itself, like who it's addressed to and whether it says "Tax Invoice". A soft failure is flagged for the manager to decide.

Two models must agree

One model reading a PDF can be confidently wrong. So two models read every invoice in parallel. If they agree, the values are checked against the record. If they disagree, a third model breaks the tie. If there is still no agreement, nothing is guessed: an internal record is raised and the team is notified. Every disagreement is logged by model, which tells us which model to trust for which kind of document. The design went through three versions on the whiteboard before it shipped.

Building for trust

Finance teams won't hand money decisions to a black box. So the reviewer always sees the PDF next to the result, every check names its rule, and the dashboard tracks people checking the AI: manual reviews, mistakes found, and AI errors by rule. Resubmitted invoices are tracked separately from first attempts.

Pricing

Charged per validated invoice, so cost follows the work the customer actually sends through.

What the pilot showed

In five weeks of watch-only mode (20 Mar – 27 Apr 2026, nothing auto-rejected) the checks ran on 2,541 invoices and flagged 210 at a hard check. Manual review found the AI wrong on about 1% of them. On 54 invoices that had already been approved or paid, the PDF and the system amount differed by AUD 17,366 in total.

What I'd do differently

Plan for two models failing at once. We hadn't, and for one day validation quietly didn't run on about 40 to 50 invoices with nobody told. Now a failed run alerts engineering and product.

Who it serves
  • Dispatcher: plans the day, reacts when a truck breaks down or a job overruns.
  • Technician: sees the next job, goes en route, works on site, completes.
  • Service manager: watches SLAs, overdue jobs and mismatches.
  • Customer: wants the visit confirmed and the job done the first time.
Design choices
  • One legend for everything: appointment status, technician status and time off types all read at a glance.
  • Time off is first class (training, sick, vacation, truck breakdown, stand-by), because a dispatcher plans around people, not slots.
  • Unassigned work sits on the left with priority and SLA breach badges, so the urgent job is the first thing you see.
  • Pricing set separately for each segment, because a retail chain and an FM company count value differently.

Launch it properly. We never ran outbound or a real launch, so the pipeline was inbound only. Good product, quiet market.

What I changed
  • Every screen says what is missing and what to do next. Empty required fields turn red, a blue bar names the button to click, and the Tab key jumps between the fields still to fill.
  • Steppers for every lifecycle. Vendor onboarding, proposals, RFQs and contracts each show where the record is and what is blocking it.
  • Contracts can't go out half-done. A contract created from a proposal lists what to fix before it can be published, like a missing billing cycle or credit note rule.
  • A home page that answers "what is waiting on us". The Command Centre shows everything that needs action, and one click opens the record.
  • Approvals in one place. They used to happen in three modules: invoices, credit notes and netted finance. Now only netted finance.
How I tested it

Contract changes in this business create invoices and credit notes, and mistakes cost real money. I ran about 250 test cases through the full life of each change and checked every record it created. Early runs flagged problems that weren't real, because the tests were missing context. Once that was fixed, they found 7 to 8 real bugs, and all of them were sorted.

How I decide an agent is ready

An agent that sounds right can still write the wrong thing into the system. So no agent of mine reaches a customer until it has passed a written set of real scenarios, and every result is checked against the record it created, not against the reply text.

Each agent has a rule registry, a scenario list, and a run log of every round. When a round finds a defect, the defect becomes a new rule and a new scenario, so it can't come back quietly.

Three lessons the evals taught me
You can't score a tool call from the chat

Once, the agent told a technician his signed sheet was filed. It wasn't. The step that should have filed it had never run. Now every round reads the record back from the system before anything is marked as passed.

A fix from one incident must say what it doesn't cover

A rule written to stop one false claim on closed jobs ended up blocking technicians from sending photos too. The fix was to split the rule clearly into what is accepted and what is refused.

"No" and "later" are different

The agent once answered a refusal with "No problem" as if the person had only postponed. Declining and deferring now have their own replies, and both are tested.

How I work

Same method, every product

I start with the person, not the feature: who does the work, what they repeat every day, and what their own records say. Then one pilot, a hard release gate, and only then scale.

No is my default

I say no when a request isn't repeatable, or doesn't add value for both the customer and the business. A customer asking for a button is usually describing a problem, so I look for the problem.

Good problems repeat

If it doesn't happen every day, it won't pay for itself. I pick problems that are already known, repeat daily, and help the customer and the business at the same time.

Success means it sticks

A launch is not the finish line. The test is whether the customer changes how they work and keeps paying for it.

Break it before customers do

Every agent I ship faces 50+ real scenarios first: emergencies, mixed requests, junk input, people trying to pull data out. It ships with known failure modes, not surprises.

Humans keep what they should

I design the handoff to people on day one, at the boundary the customer draws. AI does the repeat work. People make the calls that need judgement.

The question I stopped asking

When AI arrived, I changed one question in discovery. That one change is where Helpdesk AI and Payment Validator came from.

Before
"What do you do in your current software?"

You hear about features, screens and missing buttons. You end up building a slightly better version of the same tool.

→
After
"Walk me through your workday, morning to evening. What do you do, and in which apps?"

You hear about actions. Calls answered, emails read, invoices checked, people chased. I map those actions for each persona, and the repeated ones become candidates.

Not every problem is an AI problem

The pain is the same with or without AI

Choosing AI is a decision I make per problem, not a default. A use case has to pass a filter first, for the user and for the business. Most don't, and that's fine.

How I run my own week

PMOS is the operating system I built for myself. Every request lands as a task, every call I make is logged as a decision with its why, and anything meant to change a customer's behaviour is tracked as a bet, with a before number and an after number.

The bets are the part that keeps me honest. One example: before Payment Validator is allowed to reject invoices on its own, the customer and I agree the highest false-rejection rate we'll accept. In watch-only mode the AI was wrong on about 1% of invoices. If live rejections go above the agreed line, the system drops back to watch-only. The line is set before the switch, not after.
